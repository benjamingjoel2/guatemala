There are a handful of travel experiences that genuinely stop you in your tracks, and a night on Acatenango is one of them. From a ridge high above Antigua, you look straight across at Volcán de Fuego, one of the most active volcanoes in the world, as it rumbles, booms and throws glowing lava into the dark. It's tough, it's cold, and it's absolutely worth it.

Here's how to do it well.

## The volcano at a glance

[Acatenango](/destinations/acatenango) is a dormant stratovolcano rising to **3,976 m**, making it the third-highest peak in Guatemala. It's joined by a saddle to its twin, Fuego (around 3,760 m), which has been erupting more or less continuously since 2002. On a typical night Fuego produces small explosions every 15 to 20 minutes, with larger bursts sending lava down its flanks.

- **Trailhead:** La Soledad, around 2,400 m, about an hour's drive from Antigua
- **Base camp:** roughly 3,500–3,700 m, facing Fuego
- **Total ascent:** about 1,300–1,500 m to camp, plus around 300 m more to the summit
- **Time to camp:** typically 5–7 hours including rests
- **Duration:** 2 days, 1 night

## How hard is it, really?

Let's be honest: Acatenango is hard. The trail climbs steadily and steeply from the very first step, through farmland, cloud forest and pine woods, and then onto loose volcanic scree near the top. There are few flat sections, and the altitude makes every step a little more effortful.

That said, you don't need technical skills or mountaineering experience. If you can hike uphill for several hours with breaks, and you're comfortable with a long day on your feet, you can do it. The keys are pacing and preparation.

### Our tips for a stronger hike

1. **Acclimatise first.** Spend at least two or three nights in Antigua (1,530 m) or at Lake Atitlán before you hike.
2. **Hire a porter.** Handing over your pack transforms the climb. It's also a fair source of income for local families.
3. **Go slowly from the start.** Experienced guides set a rhythm you can sustain for hours; resist the urge to race.
4. **Eat and drink little and often.** Bring snacks you actually enjoy and around three litres of water.
5. **Use trekking poles.** They save your knees on the descent, which many people find harder than the climb.

## What to pack

Temperatures at base camp regularly drop close to freezing at night, and the wind can be biting, even when Antigua below is warm and sunny.

- **Layers:** thermal base layer, fleece, insulated jacket and waterproof shell
- **Warm extras:** hat, gloves, buff and spare thick socks
- **Footwear:** broken-in hiking boots with ankle support and good grip
- **Head torch** with spare batteries, for the pre-dawn summit push
- **Sun protection:** sunglasses, sun cream and a cap; the sun is fierce at altitude
- **Water and snacks:** around three litres, plus energy-rich food
- **Personal items:** toilet paper, hand sanitiser, any medication, and a small bag to carry out rubbish

We provide sleeping bags, mats, tents, poles and all meals, and can lend warm jackets if needed.

## What happens on the hike

### Day one: the climb

After an early pickup in Antigua, you'll drive to the trailhead and pay the small park entrance fee. The first hours wind up through cornfields and then into damp cloud forest draped in moss. After lunch, the trees thin out and the views open up. Most groups reach camp mid-afternoon, with time to rest, warm up and watch the clouds roll across the valleys below.

As darkness falls, Fuego comes alive. Each explosion lights the plume orange and red, and the sound arrives a few seconds later, rolling across the saddle. Dinner around the fire, wrapped in every layer you own, watching a volcano erupt, is one of those moments that stays with you for life.

### Day two: summit and descent

The optional summit push starts at around 4 am. It's about 60 to 90 minutes of steep, sliding scree in the dark, but you'll arrive on the crater rim as the sky starts to glow. On a clear morning you can see Agua, Pacaya, the peaks around Lake Atitlán and sometimes as far as the Pacific.

After breakfast back at camp, the descent takes around three hours. You'll be back in Antigua by early afternoon, ready for a long, hot shower.

## When to go

The **dry season from November to April** offers the clearest skies and best eruption views, with December to March being most reliable. In the **rainy season (May to October)**, mornings are often clear but afternoons cloudy and wet, and visibility at night is far less predictable. It's still possible to hike, but you need to be flexible.

> Fuego's activity varies, and the authorities occasionally restrict access after major eruptions. We monitor conditions closely and will always adjust or reschedule if the mountain isn't safe.

## Is it for you?

Acatenango isn't suitable for everyone. We don't recommend it for people with heart or serious respiratory conditions, during pregnancy, or for young children. If the full overnight hike sounds like too much, there are wonderful alternatives: Volcán Pacaya is a gentler half-day walk across fresh lava fields, and sunrise from Indian Nose at Lake Atitlán delivers huge views with a short climb.
